09 · P2P file distribution

When everyone helps out

How much faster can a peer-to-peer distribution actually be compared to client–server? Let us work it out mathematically — and see it visually.

Let us make the problem concrete. A server has a file of size F that must be distributed to N clients. The server has upload capacity us. Each client i has upload capacity ui and download capacity di. We assume the network core has ample bandwidth — the bottlenecks are at the end systems. The question is: how long does it take for all N clients to receive the file?

Client–server: linear in N

In the client–server model, the server must upload the file N times — once per client. The time for one upload is F/us, so total upload time is NF/us. At the same time, no client can receive faster than its own download speed, so the slowest client needs at least F/dmin. The total time is the larger of these two:

Client–server

Dc-s ≥ max{ NF/us , F/dmin }

The first term grows linearly with N. Double the number of clients, and the distribution time doubles.

P2P: slows down, but levels off

In P2P the calculation is different. The server still has to upload the file at least once for it to enter the system — giving a minimum of F/us. The slowest client still has to download the entire file — F/dmin. But the third term is the interesting one: the total amount of data to be delivered is NF bits, and the maximum aggregate upload capacity in the system is us + Σui. This gives:

Peer-to-peer

DP2P ≥ max{ F/us , F/dmin , NF/(us + Σui) }

The third term also grows with N, but the denominator grows too — because each new peer contributes its ui. This is what produces self-scaling.

The difference is easier to see in an example. Assume each client has upload speed u, that it takes one hour to upload the file once (F/u = 1 h), and that the server's speed is ten times a client's (us = 10u). For client–server, the dominant term becomes NF/us = N/10 — a straight line upward (until the download floor F/dmin takes over). For P2P, the third term in the bound is N/(10+N) — a curve that rises quickly toward 1 but never reaches it. The actual P2P time is still the maximum of all terms in the inequality; with the normalization F/dmin = 1 h, that floor often wins, so the effective time in the plot is a flat segment at 1 — not because the third term is irrelevant, but because it lies below the max.

When us is very large relative to the number of clients, the P2P advantage is small — because the server alone can handle it. When N grows, client–server explodes while P2P levels off. This is precisely how BitTorrent scales to millions of simultaneous downloaders.
10 · Video over an unreliable network

Streaming video, and why it is hard

Netflix, YouTube, and Amazon Prime accounted for roughly 80% of residential ISP traffic in 2020. How do you get a billion people to watch HD video simultaneously?

First, a bit about what video is. A video is simply a sequence of images displayed at a constant rate — typically 24 or 30 frames per second. Each image is a matrix of pixels, each pixel represented as a few bytes. Raw video is enormous: uncompressed HD video is gigabytes per minute. Therefore, video is always compressed before transmission, and the compression exploits two types of redundancy. Spatial redundancy within a single image: if a large area is the same color, store the color and the number of pixels instead of each individual pixel. Temporal redundancy between consecutive images: instead of sending the full image i+1, send only the differences from image i. It is this dual compression that allows MPEG-4 to squeeze HD video down to a few megabits per second.

The result of compression can be CBR (constant bit rate) — a fixed bit rate, such as MPEG-1 (1.5 Mbps) or MPEG-2 DVD (3–6 Mbps) — or VBR (variable bit rate), where the rate varies depending on how much motion and detail is in the scene. MPEG-4, which dominates on the Internet, can range from 64 kbps to 12 Mbps depending on quality and content.

The hard part: constant playback, variable network

When video is encoded and stored on a server, we need to send it to the client over a network that is anything but predictable. The bandwidth between server and client varies with congestion in the home network, the access network, the core network, and the server itself. Packets are lost and delayed. But playback at the client is strict: once playback has started, frames must arrive in the correct order and at the correct time. This is known as the continuous playout constraint.

How do you solve this? With a buffer on the client side. The client begins downloading video before it begins playing, building up a small head start. When the network temporarily becomes slow, playback draws from the buffer instead of stopping. When the network becomes fast again, the buffer refills. You have probably seen the result: "buffering..." appears only when the buffer is empty and the network is simultaneously not delivering fast enough.

DASH: let the client decide

The modern solution for streaming is called DASH — Dynamic, Adaptive Streaming over HTTP. The idea is to give all the intelligence to the client, and keep the server dumb. The server's job: divide the video into many short chunks (typically a few seconds each), encode each chunk at many different bit rates (low, medium, high, ultra), and create a manifest file that lists the URLs for all chunks at all quality levels.

The client fetches the manifest, continuously measures the bandwidth to the server, and decides for itself: which chunk should I request now, and at what quality? When bandwidth is good, it requests high-resolution chunks. When bandwidth drops, it falls to lower resolution — without pausing playback. The client can also decide where to fetch the chunk from, if multiple servers have it.

Because everything runs over ordinary HTTP, DASH benefits from the entire existing web stack: caches, firewalls, load balancers, CDNs. This is part of the reason DASH has won over older, dedicated streaming protocols.

Streaming as a recipe

Streaming video = encoding + DASH + playback buffer

11 · Content Distribution Networks

How Netflix avoids serving from one location

Even with DASH we have a problem: hundreds of thousands of simultaneous viewers demanding millions of different videos. One server — even a mega-server — cannot do it. We need to physically distribute the content.

Imagine that Netflix tried to serve the entire world from a single data center in California. It would not work. First, it would be a single point of failure — one error and the whole world loses Netflix. Second, it would create enormous congestion on the network links into the data center. Third, every user would face too long a journey to reach the content, with corresponding latency. Fourth, the same video would be sent over the same backbone links millions of times. In short: one mega-server does not scale.

The solution is a Content Distribution Network — CDN. The idea is simple: store copies of the content on many servers distributed geographically across the globe, and steer each user to a nearby server. There are two variants of the strategy.

The first is called "enter deep": push CDN servers right into many access networks, as close to users as possible. Akamai, a pioneer in the field, had around 240,000 servers in more than 120 countries in 2015. The advantage is that content sits almost on the same ISP as the user — minimal latency, minimal traffic over the Internet core. The downside is complexity: you must manage an enormous number of physical locations.

The second is called "bring home": a smaller number (tens) of larger clusters placed at POPs near — but not inside — the access networks. Limelight is an example. It is simpler to operate, but provides slightly higher latency than enter-deep.

How a user actually ends up at the right CDN server

When you click on a Netflix video, a choreographed dance takes place behind the scenes. Let us follow Bob, who wants to watch a movie stored as http://netcinema.com/6Y7B23V but actually copied out to a CDN under the domain KingCDN.com.

Bob clicks on the link from netcinema.com's website (step ①). The browser asks its local DNS which IP address netcinema.com has (②). The local DNS asks netcinema's authoritative DNS (③), but instead of receiving an IP address directly, it gets a CNAME back — an alias redirect — that points to a name under KingCDN.com (④). The local DNS must then ask KingCDN's authoritative DNS, and this server has the magic: it knows where in the world Bob is (based on which DNS request came in) and responds with the IP address of the nearest KingCDN node that has the video (⑤). Bob opens an HTTP connection to that node and streams the video (⑥).

The entire DNS acrobatics are invisible to Bob. He typed netcinema.com in the browser; he got video from a server a few kilometers away. That is the CDN magic.

OTT — over the top

A CDN like Netflix is an OTT system: "over the top." This means the content provider builds its own distribution overlay on top of the Internet, without owning the underlying lines. The Internet provides only host-to-host communication as a service — all the smart parts, all video storage, all server selection, all DASH choreography, take place at the application layer on the end systems that Netflix controls. The challenges are real: which CDN node should a given viewer be served from? How do viewers behave when the network is slow? Which content should be stored on which nodes, given that there is not enough space for everything everywhere?

Netflix and YouTube — two different strategies

Netflix uses a two-part architecture. The application logic itself — user accounts, payments, search, recommendations — runs in Amazon Web Services (AWS), that is, in the cloud. But the video is delivered from Netflix's own CDN, called Open Connect. Open Connect follows an "enter deep" strategy: Netflix places dedicated servers (called OCAs) directly inside ISPs' networks around the world. When you press "play" in the Netflix app, Netflix's software (via AWS) decides which OCA is best for you — based on network proximity and capacity — and you stream the video from there with DASH over HTTP.

YouTube uses Google's own private CDN, with a combination of large data center clusters and many smaller locations closer to users. An important difference from Netflix is that YouTube relies more on pull-based caching: not all content is pre-placed everywhere. Popular content naturally ends up on many nodes because it is frequently requested, while rarely watched content may only exist in a central data center. YouTube also uses DNS redirection to send users to the right cluster, but the entire process takes place within Google's own infrastructure.

The common denominator
Both Netflix and YouTube are OTT services that do not own the access networks users are on — they build their own distribution infrastructure on top of the public Internet. Both use DASH and HTTP for the actual streaming. But Netflix actively pre-places content (push), while YouTube relies more on caching content reactively when it becomes popular (pull).
What are the advantages and disadvantages of "enter deep" vs "bring home" as a CDN strategy? When would you choose one over the other?

Enter deep (the Akamai model) places servers right inside the access networks, close to the end user. The advantage is minimal latency and less traffic over the Internet core. The downside is enormous operational complexity — thousands of locations that must be maintained. Bring home (the Limelight model) uses fewer but larger clusters placed at POPs near the access networks. The advantage is simpler operations; the downside is slightly higher latency. Enter deep is best suited for latency-critical content such as live video where milliseconds matter. Bring home is suitable for content where somewhat higher latency is acceptable, and where operational simplicity and cost control are more important.
