Chapter 7 · Data Communications

Wireless and Mobile Networks

For most people in the world today, the Internet is wireless. This chapter is about why it is so much harder to send bits through air than through a cable — and what happens when the receiver is also on the move.

Big Picture

Like a conversation at a noisy party

The entire chapter boils down to two challenges: being heard, and staying connected while you move.

Think about the difference between talking to someone through a tube versus shouting at them across a crowded party. Through the tube — a cable — the communication is private, clear, and undisturbed. You know the receiver hears you, and no one else interferes. But the wireless world is the party: your voice gets weaker with distance, others are shouting in the background on the same frequency, the sound reflects off walls and arrives as echoes, and if you walk into another room the person suddenly can't hear you. All of this is the physics behind wireless communication — signal loss, interference, and multipath propagation.

But the chapter doesn't stop there. There is a second challenge that is entirely independent of radio physics: mobility. What happens when you're not just standing at the party, but wandering from room to room — and expecting the conversation to continue uninterrupted? The network must route your voice to the right room at all times, without you noticing. That is exactly what mobile networks do: they build tunnels that can be moved in the background, so that your IP address remains the same regardless of which base station you are currently talking to.

Two challenges — one about physics, one about networking

Challenge 1 — Wireless communication: How do we get bits across a radio link where the signal is attenuated, interfered with, and reflected? The solutions are CSMA/CA (avoid collisions instead of detecting them), rate adaptation (reduce speed when the signal weakens), and RTS/CTS (reserve the channel when hidden terminals can cause damage).

Challenge 2 — Mobility: What happens when the device that is communicating moves between base stations, networks, or countries? The solution is tunneling (GTP) — packets are wrapped inside packets, so that the endpoint can be moved without changing the IP address and breaking the TCP connection.

When you watch a YouTube video on the bus and it keeps playing even as the bus passes three different cell towers — that is mobility in action. Your phone switches base stations, the GTP tunnel is moved, but the P-GW (the gateway to the Internet) remains the same. The TCP connection notices nothing because the IP address never changes. It is an elegant piece of engineering that we take completely for granted, but which requires an entire architecture of tunnels, databases, and coordination behind the scenes.

WiFi solves the wireless problem with CSMA/CA — a collision avoidance strategy where nodes listen before sending and confirm receipt with ACK. Cellular networks (4G/5G) take an entirely different approach: the base station allocates time slots centrally instead of letting nodes compete, and builds a complete core network with authentication, mobility management, and tunneling on top. Both solve the same fundamental problem — sending bits reliably through air — but with entirely different philosophies.

Test Yourself

Check whether you have understood the key concepts from this chapter.

Question 1
What is the difference between "wireless" and "mobile"?
A smart TV on WiFi is wireless but not mobile. A tablet with a USB cable can be mobile but not wireless. The two problems — radio communication and mobility management — are solved independently of each other.
Question 2
Why does WiFi (802.11) use CSMA/CA instead of CSMA/CD like Ethernet does?
On a radio link, the sender's own signal is millions of times stronger than incoming signals, so the receiver is "blind" while transmitting. Additionally, the hidden terminal problem means two senders can collide at a third party without either of them knowing it. That is why WiFi must avoid collisions (CA) instead of detecting them (CD).
Question 3
Why does 4G LTE use GTP tunneling instead of regular IP routing through the core network?
Regular IP routing binds addresses to subnets. Without tunneling, a base station switch would require a new IP address, and all TCP connections would break. GTP tunnels let the P-GW (the gateway) own the IP address, while only the tunnel endpoint is updated during handover — an elegant form of indirection.
Question 4
What is the "hidden terminal problem" in wireless networks?
The hidden terminal problem occurs when nodes A and C are both within range of B, but not of each other. Both listen, hear silence, and transmit — but collide at B. CSMA alone does not solve this; it requires mechanisms like RTS/CTS where the base station reserves the channel on behalf of the sender.
Question 5
What is the most important practical difference between the 5G frequency bands FR1 (under 6 GHz) and FR2 (millimeter waves, 24–52 GHz)?
FR2 (mmWave) offers up to 10 Gbps, but the signal is blocked by almost anything — walls, raindrops, human bodies. The range is typically 10–100 meters, which requires massive deployment of small pico cells. FR1 is more like traditional cellular frequencies with good range but moderate speeds.
