Chapter 7 · Data Communications

Wireless and Mobile Networks

How a radio signal fragment bouncing between WiFi routers, cell towers, and mountainsides becomes a TCP connection that doesn't break when you walk around a corner.

01 · Introduction

Why is wireless so hard?

Two problems that are often conflated, but are actually different.

It is easy to forget how radically the world has changed. In 2019, there were ten times more mobile subscriptions than landline phones, and five times more mobile broadband connections than fixed-line broadband. For most people in the world today, the internet is wireless. Yet when we study data communications, we almost always start with cables. That is because cables are simple: a wire from A to B, a signal that doesn't change much along the way, and nobody suddenly moving around.

Wireless gives us two new, distinct challenges that are worth separating right away:

Challenge 1 — Wireless communication

How do we get bits across a radio link at all? The signal gets weaker with distance, it is interfered with by other sources on the same frequency, it reflects off walls and the ground and arrives multiple times with slightly different delays. This is physics and signal processing.

Challenge 2 — Mobility

What happens when the node that is communicating actually moves? It must be able to switch base stations mid-stream, and perhaps even switch to an entirely different operator network in another country — without the TCP connection breaking. This is a networking problem.

An important point: wireless does not automatically mean mobile. A smart TV hanging on WiFi in your living room is wireless but never mobile. A piece of industrial IoT equipment on an oil platform is wireless but bolted down. Conversely, a tablet is mobile even when connected via a USB cable — you can carry it around. We therefore need to solve the two problems independently, even though they most often occur together.

In this chapter, the focus is primarily on challenge 1 — how wireless links work, how WiFi coordinates many devices on the same frequency, and how cellular networks (4G/5G) build an architecture around the fact that users are constantly on the move.

02 · Components

Building Blocks

Four components that appear in nearly every wireless system, from home WiFi to 5G.

Before we dive into the protocols, we need to agree on the names of things. There are four terms that appear over and over again:

Wireless host

A device that runs applications — laptop, smartphone, IoT sensor, smart refrigerator. It is the client in the traditional sense. It may be stationary or moving; the word "host" says nothing about mobility.

Base station

A base station is the bridge between the wireless and the wired. It is typically connected to a wired network on one side, and communicates via radio with the hosts within its coverage area on the other. It functions as a relay: takes packets from the wired network and transmits them over radio, and vice versa. The WiFi access point on the ceiling at home is a base station. So is the cell tower on top of the apartment building. Both do the same job, just at different scales.

Wireless link

The actual radio connection between the host and the base station. Links vary enormously in speed, range, and frequency band — from Bluetooth that reaches a few meters at 2 Mbps, to 5G that can theoretically reach 10 Gbps over short distances. An important common denominator: all wireless links are shared media, so we need a multiple access protocol to coordinate who gets to send when.

The infrastructure behind

The "wired network behind" that the base stations are connected to. For a home WiFi, it is the broadband router and fiber cable. For 4G, it is an entirely separate network of routers, gateways, and databases that we will return to shortly.

A selection of wireless links

03 · Taxonomy

With or without infrastructure

Two dimensions divide wireless networks into four families.

When we try to organize the jungle of wireless technologies, it helps to ask two things: (1) is there a base station, and (2) do packets need to hop through multiple nodes to reach the destination? These two yes/no questions give us four categories:

Single hopMultiple hops
With infrastructure Classic WiFi, cellular networks. The host talks directly with a base station, which forwards to the Internet. Mesh networks: some nodes do not talk directly with the base station, but relay through other nodes.
Without infrastructure Bluetooth, ad hoc WiFi mode. Directly between two nodes, no connection to the wider Internet. MANET, VANET (vehicle-to-vehicle). Packets must be routed through multiple ad hoc nodes.

The vast majority of what we encounter in daily life sits in the upper left corner — single hop, with infrastructure. That is the simplest variant, and it is what the rest of this chapter is about.

04 · Link Properties

What makes a wireless link fundamentally different?

Three physical phenomena that do not exist on a cable.

If a cable is a quiet hallway where you shout to your neighbor and hear a clear reply, then a wireless link is more like a cramped table at a noisy restaurant. Your voice gets weaker the farther away the receiver sits. Others are talking simultaneously on the same frequency. And the sound can echo back from the walls. Three phenomena — signal loss, interference, and multipath propagation — make even a simple point-to-point radio connection harder than an equivalent cable.

1. Path loss

A radio signal gets weaker as it travels through air, walls, bodies, and furniture. In open space, the signal power drops approximately with the square of the distance — double the distance, and the signal becomes one quarter as strong. Inside a building with concrete walls, the attenuation is much worse. That is why your WiFi signal is often dead two rooms away from the router.

2. Interference from other sources

Many devices share the same frequency bands. The 2.4 GHz band is used not only by WiFi, but also by Bluetooth, microwave ovens, baby monitors, and certain wireless mice. Your neighbor's WiFi on the same channel also becomes interference for your network. Each of these sources adds "noise" that the receiver must try to decode the correct signal through.

3. Multipath propagation

A radio signal does not travel only in a straight line. It reflects off walls, floors, cars, and buildings, and therefore arrives at the receiver multiple times via different paths — all with slightly different delays. The result is that signal waves overlap and can both reinforce and cancel each other, depending on whether they are in phase or not. This creates unpredictable "dead spots" that can shift when someone opens a door.

SNR, BER, and rate adaptation

The result of all this is that we must talk about signal relative to noise: SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio), usually in decibels. The higher the SNR, the easier it is for the receiver to extract the correct bit stream from the signal. Low SNR means higher BER (Bit Error Rate) — more bits are misinterpreted.

Here is the important insight: we can choose how robust to make the signal. The sender can choose a modulation method that packs many bits into each symbol (e.g., QAM256, which gives 8 Mbps), or a more conservative method (BPSK, which only gives 1 Mbps but tolerates much lower SNR without errors). This trade-off — high speed vs. robustness — is the heart of rate adaptation: when SNR drops because you move farther from the router, the sender automatically switches to a lower but more robust modulation. That is why your WiFi speed doesn't just "disconnect" when you walk away, but gradually becomes slower first.

05 · Multi-user Problems

Hidden terminals and fading

Two problems that only arise when multiple nodes talk simultaneously — and that "listen before you send" cannot solve alone.

When we have only one sender and one receiver, wireless is actually manageable. It is when multiple nodes must share the same medium that it gets interesting. We have already seen this problem for wired networks (Ethernet, chapter 6), but wireless has a couple of extra variants that are worth understanding thoroughly.

The hidden terminal problem

Consider three nodes: A, B, and C. B sits in the middle. A and B can hear each other fine. B and C can also hear each other. But between A and C there is a mountain, a concrete wall, or just too much distance — so A and C cannot hear each other at all.

If A and C now both decide to send to B at the same time, they will not detect that they are doing so. Both "listen on the channel," both hear silence (because the other is invisible to them), and both transmit. The result is a collision at B — but neither A nor C knows it happened, because they cannot hear each other's signals. This is the classic hidden terminal problem, and it means that "listen before you send" (CSMA) is not enough in the wireless world.

Signal fading

The second variant is similar but has a different cause. Consider three nodes in a line — A, B, C — without any mountain between them. The distance is just large enough that A's signal is strong enough to reach B but has attenuated to nothing before reaching C, and vice versa for C. The result is the same: A and C "see" nothing of each other and can transmit simultaneously without knowing it. Both signals collide at B in the middle.

Both problems have the same consequence: we cannot rely on the "listen before you send" strategy alone. We need something else, and that is where CSMA/CA and the RTS/CTS mechanism in WiFi come in.

Imagine we could magically listen while transmitting on a wireless link (i.e., that CSMA/CD were technically possible). Would that solve the hidden terminal problem?
No. Even if A could listen while transmitting, it would still not hear C — because C is out of range. Collision detection requires that the sender hears all other potential senders, but in a wireless network, that is precisely what is not the case. The hidden terminal problem is not about being unable to listen and transmit simultaneously, but about two senders being invisible to each other. That is why an explicit reservation mechanism like RTS/CTS is needed, where the base station — which can hear both — conveys the message.

06 · 802.11

WiFi: 802.11 in Detail

The dominant standard for wireless local area networks — one family, many generations.

IEEE 802.11 has been the clear winner among standards for wireless local area networks since the turn of the millennium. It is not because it is technically perfect — it has some odd corners, as we shall soon see — but because it is open, cheap, and good enough. Each new generation (b, g, n, ac, ax) has remained backward compatible and raised speeds by an order of magnitude or two, building on the same basic architecture.

Architecture: BSS, AP, and infrastructure mode

The fundamental building block of a WiFi network is called the Basic Service Set (BSS), or simply "cell." A BSS consists of an access point (AP) — the base station — and the wireless hosts that have associated with this access point. The AP is in turn connected to a switch or router that leads out to the Internet. A home WiFi typically has one BSS. A larger office space has many BSSes — one per AP — that together cover the building.

In addition to this infrastructure mode, there is an ad hoc mode where hosts talk directly with each other without any AP. It is rarely used in practice, so we focus on infrastructure mode.

Channels and frequencies

The WiFi spectrum is divided into channels at different frequencies. In the 2.4 GHz band there are 11–14 channels (depending on the country), and considerably more in the 5 GHz band. When you set up an access point, the administrator (or the AP automatically) chooses which channel to transmit on. The problem is that a neighbor's AP may have chosen the same channel — and then the two networks must share the airspace, with interference as the result.

Association: how does a device join a network?

When you bring your laptop into a building with WiFi, it must find an AP and connect to it. This happens in three or four steps, and there are two variants:

Passive scanning

  • The AP broadcasts beacon frames about 10 times per second with its name (SSID) and MAC address
  • The host listens on all channels for such beacons
  • Selects one AP and sends an association request
  • AP replies with an association confirmation

Active scanning

  • The host broadcasts a probe request itself
  • All APs within range reply with a probe response
  • The host selects one and sends an association request
  • AP replies with an association confirmation

After association comes authentication (your password is verified) and then DHCP to get an IP address in the AP's subnet. Only now are you actually on the Internet.

Rate adaptation

An important advanced feature of 802.11 is rate adaptation — the WiFi transmitter automatically switches between different modulation methods depending on signal conditions. When you are sitting right next to the router, it uses an aggressive modulation (e.g., QAM256) that provides high speed. When you move farther away and SNR drops, it shifts down to a more robust but slower modulation. This is why WiFi speed gradually decreases the farther you are from the AP, instead of just cutting out abruptly. We described the physics behind this in section 04 — the slider there illustrates the relationship between distance, SNR, and chosen modulation.

When your phone "finds" all WiFi networks nearby in the settings menu, active scanning is what is happening — it broadcasts probes and collects all the responses.
07 · Multiple Access

CSMA/CA: Collision Avoidance

Why 802.11 cannot detect collisions, and what it does instead.

In Ethernet (cable) we use CSMA/CD: listen before you send, and if you detect a collision while sending, stop immediately and try again later. This works because an Ethernet NIC can listen and transmit simultaneously on the same wire, and thereby compare what it is sending with what is actually on the line.

In wireless, this is practically impossible for two reasons:

  1. Your own signal is enormously strong compared to signals you receive from others. When you transmit, you completely "deafen" your own receiver. It is like shouting in a room and simultaneously trying to hear if someone else is shouting — you only hear yourself.
  2. Even if you could listen while transmitting, you would still not catch all collisions. The hidden terminal problem means two nodes can collide at a third without either of them noticing.

The conclusion is simple: 802.11 cannot detect collisions, so it must avoid them in the first place. That is why it is called CSMA/CA — Collision Avoidance, not Collision Detection.

How CSMA/CA works

The algorithm follows a few rules. When a node wants to send:

The CSMA/CA protocol

1. Listen on the channel. If it is idle for a time period called DIFS (Distributed Inter-Frame Space), send the entire frame.

2. If the channel is busy, choose a random backoff time and start a countdown. The counter decrements when the channel is quiet, and freezes when someone else is sending. When the counter reaches zero, send.

3. The receiver waits a short time (SIFS, shorter than DIFS) and sends an ACK. SIFS is shorter than DIFS so that the ACK always gets to send first, before any new node tries to start a transmission.

4. If the sender does not receive an ACK, it doubles the backoff window and tries again. This is exponential backoff, the same idea as in Ethernet.

Note that the ACK is important here. On cable, we can usually trust that a sent frame is also received correctly — Ethernet has no link-layer ACK. On wireless, there are far too many things that can go wrong (signal fading, hidden terminals, interference), so each frame must be confirmed explicitly.

All nodes that receive a CTS addressed to someone else know they must remain silent for a certain period, and thus the collision that would otherwise result from a hidden terminal is avoided. The mechanism is typically used only for large frames where the overhead is worth it.

08 · The 802.11 Frame

The Frame Format and the Four Addresses

Why a WiFi frame has room for four MAC addresses when Ethernet only needs two.

When you look at an Ethernet frame format, it has two MAC addresses: source and destination. That's it. An 802.11 frame, however, has four address fields, and it is confusing at first glance. It is worth understanding why.

Why four addresses?

Consider the simplest case: host H1 (on WiFi) wants to send a packet out to the Internet. The packet must go H1 → AP → router R1 → Internet. On the wireless hop itself, there are three relevant MAC addresses:

  • Address 1 — Receiver of this frame: The AP's MAC. That is who should pick up the frame from the air.
  • Address 2 — Sender of this frame: H1's MAC. That is who actually transmitted it.
  • Address 3 — Final destination on the wired network: R1's MAC. The AP needs this to know who to forward to after the frame arrives.
  • Address 4: Used in ad hoc mode and when two APs forward to each other wirelessly (WDS — Wireless Distribution System). In normal infrastructure mode, it is empty.

The sequence number — more important than you think

In addition to the four addresses, the 802.11 frame has a sequence number field. Why? Because the ACK mechanism in CSMA/CA creates a special problem: what happens if the sender sends a frame, the receiver receives it and sends an ACK, but the ACK is lost? The sender gets no confirmation and resends the frame — but the receiver has already delivered it up to the network layer. Without a sequence number, the receiver could not distinguish a retransmission from a completely new frame, and would deliver duplicate data up the stack. The sequence number lets the receiver recognize and discard such duplicates.

When the AP receives the 802.11 frame, it "unwraps" the contents (the IP packet) and creates a new Ethernet frame. In the Ethernet frame, the source is H1's MAC and the destination is R1's MAC — because when sending on the wired network, no intermediate AP address is needed. The AP thus functions as a bridge between the two link protocols.

We saw the addresses for the direction H1 → AP → R1. What happens to the four address fields when a packet goes the opposite direction — from the Internet, through R1, via the AP and out to H1 over WiFi?
In the wireless frame from AP to H1, the roles are reversed: address 1 (receiver of the frame) is now H1's MAC, address 2 (sender of the frame) is the AP's MAC, and address 3 (original sender on the wired network) is R1's MAC. The AP thus does the reverse translation: it takes the Ethernet frame from R1, unwraps the IP packet, and creates a new 802.11 frame with the correct addresses for the wireless hop.
09 · Mobility

When the host moves between APs

What actually happens in the background when you walk from one WiFi room to another?

Imagine that H1 — your laptop — is associated with AP-1 in one room. You walk into another room where AP-2 has better coverage. What must happen for your TCP connection to survive the switch?

The simple scenario is mobility within the same IP subnet. If both APs are connected to the same switch and are therefore on the same subnet, H1 can keep its IP address. The only change that must happen is that the switch must learn that H1 is now reached via a different port. Fortunately, the switch does this completely automatically — it is the same self-learning mechanism we saw in chapter 6: when the switch sees a frame arrive from H1 via the new port, it updates its table and henceforth forwards traffic to H1 there.

The roaming itself — the fact that H1 disassociates from AP-1 and associates with AP-2 — is handled at the 802.11 layer and is invisible to the IP stack above. The TCP connection notices nothing.

The difficult case is when you need to move between different IP subnets, or worse, between different operator networks. Here, genuine mobility management is needed — and that is precisely what lies as a core service in cellular networks (4G/5G), which we will look at now.

Power saving: a small but important detail

WiFi nodes use a lot of power on the radio. For battery-powered devices (phones, IoT), being able to sleep is important. The mechanism is simple: the node tells the AP that it will sleep until the next beacon frame. The AP buffers any packets that arrive in the meantime. The beacon frames from the AP contain a list of which sleeping nodes have pending traffic — so when a node wakes up and checks the beacon, it immediately knows whether it needs to stay awake or can go back to sleep.

10 · Cellular Networks

4G LTE: an entirely separate architecture

What a global cellular network looks like from the inside — and why it is so different from WiFi.

WiFi is simple because it mostly only cares about one access point at a time and leaves all the hard stuff to the Internet behind it. Cellular networks cannot do that. They must handle billions of users who are constantly moving between cells, between networks, between countries — while simultaneously billing correctly, ensuring calls don't drop, and guaranteeing that a call in Norway actually reaches a recipient in Brazil. The result is an architecture that is far more ambitious than WiFi.

Similarities and differences from the wired Internet

Similar to the Internet

  • Edge/core separation, all owned by the same operator
  • A global "network of networks"
  • Uses many of the same protocols: HTTP, DNS, TCP, UDP, IP, NAT
  • Data/control plane separation, SDN concepts
  • Connects to the regular Internet

Different

  • Entirely different link layer (radio instead of Ethernet)
  • Mobility is a first-class service, not an afterthought
  • User identity resides in the SIM card, not in a MAC address
  • Business model: strong distinction between "home network" and "roaming"
  • Inter-operator settlement and global authentication

Main components of 4G LTE

4G LTE consists of two main parts: the radio access network (everything between the phone and the base station) and the Evolved Packet Core (EPC) — the mobile operator's core network, which is entirely IP-based. Inside the EPC are a handful of specialized components whose names you need to know:

UE — User Equipment

The mobile device itself. Phone, tablet, IoT module. Globally identified by a 64-bit IMSI (International Mobile Subscriber Identity) stored on the SIM card. It is the IMSI, not the phone number, that is the "real" identity in the mobile network.

eNode-B — The Base Station

LTE's version of a WiFi AP, but much more active. It not only manages the radio resources in its own cell, but also coordinates with neighboring cells to optimize spectrum usage, and plays an active role in mobility (handover between cells). A home WiFi AP does essentially none of this — it is just a passive bridge.

MME — Mobility Management Entity

The brain of the control plane. It authenticates the device, manages which cell it is currently in, handles handover between cells, and tracks the device even when it is inactive (so it can be paged when someone calls). It also sets up the tunnel between the phone and the P-GW.

HSS — Home Subscriber Server

A central database containing information about all subscribers who have this operator as their home network: what services you have, which keys belong to your SIM card, which rules apply. When the MME needs to authenticate your phone, it talks to the HSS to verify the SIM card. When you roam abroad, the visited network talks to the HSS at your home operator.

S-GW and P-GW — Gateways

The two gateways sit on the data plane, i.e., on the actual data path from the phone to the Internet. S-GW (Serving Gateway) is the local endpoint for data traffic — where packets from base stations are collected. P-GW (PDN Gateway) is the exit to the regular Internet, and functions like a router with NAT, dynamic IP assignment, and packet filtering. From the outside, the P-GW looks like any other Internet gateway. Your IP address on the Internet is the P-GW's.

Separation of data and control planes

An important architectural choice in LTE is that data and control travel different paths. The control plane (authentication, mobility management, connection setup) uses the MME and HSS. The data plane (actual browser traffic, video streaming, etc.) never passes through the MME — it goes straight through base station → S-GW → P-GW → Internet. This is the same idea as SDN: a centralized controller tells the switches what to do, while the packets themselves flow through at full speed.

11 · LTE Data Plane

Tunnels all the way down

Why LTE wraps IP packets inside other IP packets, and what that has to do with mobility.

The first hop — from the phone to the base station — is wireless and uses LTE's own protocol stack. What matters is what happens from there into the operator's core network. This is the most interesting (and confusing) part of LTE.

Link-layer protocols on the first hop

Between the phone and the base station is a complete set of protocol layers that do not exist on the regular Internet. From top to bottom:

  • Packet Data Convergence Protocol (PDCP): compresses IP headers and encrypts user data. Header compression is important because a TCP/IP header is often 40 bytes — as large as a short voice packet, so without compression much of the capacity would go to overhead.
  • Radio Link Control (RLC): breaks packets into smaller fragments that fit the radio time slots, and handles reliable transmission (retransmissions) at the link layer.
  • Medium Access Control (MAC): decides which radio time slot to use when. Unlike WiFi, there is no "listen before you send" here — the base station allocates time slots to each device according to a scheduling algorithm (which is not standardized; each operator can use its own).

The physical modulation is called OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing). The idea is to split a large frequency band into many narrower, "orthogonal" sub-channels that do not interfere with each other. Each active device is allocated two or more 0.5 ms time slots across 12 frequencies. The combination of FDM and TDM allows many devices to share the same cell without stepping on each other.

Tunneling: why packets are inside packets

Here comes the truly elegant part. When a data packet from your phone arrives at the base station, it is not simply forwarded straight to the S-GW. Instead, it is wrapped in a new IP packet using a protocol called GTP (GPRS Tunneling Protocol), and this new packet is sent in a UDP datagram to the S-GW. The S-GW unwraps the GTP, creates a new GTP tunnel onward to the P-GW, and there the packet is finally unwrapped and sent out on the regular Internet.

Why all this wrapping? The answer is mobility. If your phone hops from one base station to another, the S-GW and P-GW do not need to know or care — only the endpoints of the GTP tunnel are updated. The phone's IP address remains the same at all times (it is assigned by the P-GW), and all packets continue to flow through the same P-GW. The only thing that changes is which base station is the lower end of the tunnel. That is the entire reason you can watch a YouTube video without interruption while sitting on a train that passes dozens of cells.

How the phone finds a network

When the phone turns on, it must find a base station to associate with. This happens roughly like this:

  1. Each base station broadcasts a primary synchronization signal every 5 milliseconds on all frequencies it supports. This is like a lighthouse — the phone does not yet know what it has found, but it can detect the signal.
  2. When the phone finds a primary synchronization signal, it looks for a secondary synchronization signal on the same frequency. This provides more information about the base station — channel width, configuration, and which operator it belongs to.
  3. The phone may detect signals from multiple base stations — including from different operators — and selects the best one (typically the one from the home operator, or the one with the strongest signal).
  4. After selection come several more steps: authentication with MME/HSS, state setup, tunnel establishment. Only now is the phone ready to send real data.

Power saving: light and deep sleep

Just like WiFi, LTE has power-saving mechanisms. After a few hundred milliseconds without traffic, the radio enters light sleep and wakes periodically to check for incoming data. After 5–10 seconds of inactivity, it enters deep sleep, and may even move between cells while sleeping. When it wakes from deep sleep, the entire association must be re-established — that is why you sometimes notice a small delay the first time you do something on your phone after it has been idle for a while.

Roaming: how gateways handle you being abroad

When you take your phone to another country, you are no longer in your home network. You are still identified by your SIM card, and it still indicates which network is home. The visited network puts you on its own P-GW (or sometimes routes traffic home first), and talks to your home operator's HSS to verify your identity and determine what services you are entitled to. It is this mechanism — together with inter-operator settlement — that makes your phone "just work" when you step off the plane in Tokyo.

Why do mobile operators use GTP tunneling instead of simply routing IP packets directly through the core network, the way the regular Internet does?
Regular IP routing binds an IP address to a specific subnet. If the phone switches base stations (and thus subnets), it would normally need a new IP address — and then all ongoing TCP connections would break. GTP tunneling solves this by separating the phone's IP address from its physical location. The P-GW owns the IP address and is always the endpoint as seen from the Internet. Packets are tunneled down to the correct base station regardless of where the phone is. During handover, only the tunnel endpoint is updated — the IP address and TCP connections remain untouched. It is fundamentally a form of indirection: "always call the P-GW, and the P-GW knows where the phone is right now."
12 · 5G

What is new with 5G?

Three promises, and the physical trade-off behind them.

5G was marketed as a revolution, and while much of the marketing was noise, there are real improvements. The three official goals were: 10x higher maximum bitrate than 4G, 10x lower latency, and 100x more capacity per square kilometer. How do you achieve that?

New frequency bands: FR1 and FR2

5G NR (New Radio) uses two different types of frequency bands:

  • FR1 (450 MHz – 6 GHz): an extended version of traditional cellular frequencies. These travel far and penetrate walls, but do not provide dramatically higher speeds than 4G. Most of what we call "5G" today uses FR1.
  • FR2 (24–52 GHz, "millimeter waves"): this is where the really large bandwidth lies, and it is these frequencies that deliver up to 10 Gbps. But they have a major problem: the signal tolerates almost nothing. A wall, a raindrop, a human body — everything blocks mmWave. The range is typically 10–100 meters.

MIMO and beamforming

To get more out of a given frequency spectrum, 5G uses MIMO (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output) at a large scale — multiple antennas on both the sender and receiver sides, which can steer beams in specific directions and send parallel data streams to different users simultaneously. This requires sophisticated signal processing but provides both higher speed and more capacity in the same cell.

Pico cells and massive deployment

Because the mmWave signal practically doesn't reach anywhere, real 5G coverage requires massive deployment of small base stations — so-called "pico cells" with a coverage radius of 10–100 meters. It is necessary to place one at every street corner, every lamppost, every building. That is the main reason "real 5G" has been slow to roll out and is limited to densely built areas with high usage pressure.

Not backward compatible

5G NR is not backward compatible with 4G LTE at the radio layer. Operators therefore run both networks in parallel, and the phone automatically chooses which one to use depending on what signals it detects and what it needs to do. Within the core, there is also a new architecture (5G Core, or 5GC) that takes the concept of data/control separation and microservices even further than EPC did, but the same fundamental ideas still apply.

Summary

5G is not one technology but a family of improvements: more spectrum (especially mmWave), smarter antennas (MIMO/beamforming), denser cell deployment, and a core network that is even more programmable than 4G's. The physical trade-off is that the most spectacular speeds require an entirely new deployment at a scale we have never seen before.
