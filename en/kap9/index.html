Multimedia Networking

How audio and video travel over a network that really only promises "best effort" — and what happens when the packets don't arrive on time.

The big picture

A live concert through the postal system

The Internet was designed for data that tolerates delay. Multimedia requires something entirely different — and this entire chapter is about how we bridge that gap.

Imagine you are going to hold a live concert, but the musicians are scattered across the country — and the only way to communicate is the postal system. Every sheet of music you send arrives, but some letters take one day, others take five. For ordinary correspondence, that is unproblematic. But for the musicians, it is a disaster: if the drum note arrives three bars late, the entire piece sounds terrible. That is the problem with multimedia over the Internet. The network only guarantees "best effort" — the packets will probably arrive eventually, but no one promises when. For email and web pages, that does not matter. For video and voice, timing is everything.

The solution is surprisingly elegant: we cannot fix the network (it will always have variable delay), but we can build smart mechanisms in the endpoints. The most important idea is the playout buffer — a temporary store at the receiver that collects packets and plays them out at a steady rate, even though they arrived at uneven intervals. It is as if the concert conductor waits to start the piece until enough sheets of music have arrived, so the musicians always have the next note ready.

Why Netflix suddenly gets pixelated

When you are watching Netflix and the picture suddenly becomes pixelated, it is because DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP) has switched to a lower quality because the network got slower. Your client requests video segments at the resolution it believes the network can handle right now. If it gets better again, it switches back to HD. All of this happens automatically, without you pressing anything — and it is a direct result of the techniques in this chapter.

The chapter covers the entire spectrum: from how analog audio and video become a bit stream (sampling, compression, CBR vs. VBR), through streaming architectures (HTTP/TCP vs. UDP, DASH), to the special challenges of real-time conversations (VoIP). There we learn that delay over 400 ms ruins a phone call, that jitter is more dangerous than average delay, and that clever techniques like FEC (Forward Error Correction) and interleaving allow us to recover lost packets without waiting for retransmission. Finally, we meet RTP — the protocol that gives multimedia packets what UDP lacks: timestamps, sequence numbers, and type identification.

Test yourself

Check whether you have understood the most important concepts from this chapter.

Question 1 · Easy
Which statement best describes the difference between streaming and full download?
Streaming starts playback before the full file is received by using continuous buffering.
Question 2 · Medium
In practice, which statement about the role of the playout buffer is most correct?
The playout buffer smooths uneven packet arrivals to keep playback steady.
Question 3 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of the tradeoff of a larger buffer is most accurate?
A larger buffer tolerates more jitter but increases end-to-end delay.
Question 4 · Medium
In practice, which statement about what jitter means is most correct?
Jitter is variation in packet delay, not necessarily high average delay.
Question 5 · Easy
Which statement best describes DASH client bitrate selection?
A DASH client selects segment quality dynamically based on network measurements and buffer level.
Question 6 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of segment length in adaptive video is most accurate?
Shorter segments adapt faster but increase protocol and request overhead.
Question 7 · Medium
In practice, which statement about CDNs for multimedia is most correct?
A CDN reduces delay and backbone load by serving media from nearby edge nodes.
Question 8 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of RTP sequence numbers is most accurate?
RTP sequence numbers are used to detect loss and handle reordering at the receiver.
Question 9 · Easy
Which statement best describes RTP timestamps?
RTP timestamps help receivers place media correctly on the playback timeline.
Question 10 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of RTCP feedback is most accurate?
RTCP reports quality metrics such as loss and jitter for adaptive stream control.
Question 11 · Medium
In practice, which statement about delay bounds for natural voice conversation is most correct?
Roughly below 150 ms feels natural for conversation; higher delay makes dialogue harder.
Question 12 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of what happens beyond 400 ms voice delay is most accurate?
At very high delay, participants talk over each other and conversation flow degrades.
Question 13 · Easy
Which statement best describes why UDP is often used for real-time media?
UDP avoids retransmission latency and lets applications directly control loss-time tradeoffs.
Question 14 · Medium
In practice, which statement about a drawback of TCP for live media is most correct?
TCP retransmissions and head-of-line blocking can add unacceptable delay to live media.
Question 15 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of FEC in media streams is most accurate?
FEC adds redundancy so receivers can recover losses without retransmission.
Question 16 · Medium
In practice, which statement about interleaving is most correct?
Interleaving spreads burst losses over time so impairments are less noticeable.
Question 17 · Easy
Which statement best describes video QoE factors?
Startup time, rebuffering, visual quality, and quality switches strongly affect user experience.
Question 18 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of adaptive bitrate under poor networks is most accurate?
When capacity drops, the client should lower bitrate early to avoid playback stalls.
Question 19 · Medium
In practice, which statement about low-latency live streaming is most correct?
Low-latency setups use smaller buffers and shorter segments but tolerate less network variation.
Question 20 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of I/P/B frames in video coding is most accurate?
I-frames provide reference points, while P/B-frames improve compression via temporal prediction.
Question 21 · Medium
In practice, which statement about keyframe interval is most correct?
A shorter keyframe interval improves seeking and robustness but requires more bitrate.
Question 22 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of lip-sync is most accurate?
Audio and video must be synchronized with timestamps and clock handling for natural playback.
Question 23 · Easy
Which statement best describes multicast in distribution networks?
Multicast can scale one-to-many efficiently where infrastructure supports it.
Question 24 · Medium
In practice, which statement about why OTT often uses unicast is most correct?
Unicast works across today's Internet without requiring global multicast support.
Question 25 · Hard
In a larger network setting, which evaluation of pacing and congestion control in media delivery is most accurate?
Smooth pacing and congestion-aware control reduce bursts, loss, and arrival variance.
