01 · The starting point

The problem the control plane solves

Before we talk about algorithms and protocols: why do we even need a "control plane"?

Imagine you are sending an email from your laptop in Trondheim to a friend in Tokyo. Your packet must pass through dozens of routers — perhaps a hundred — before it arrives. At every single router along the way, someone must make a decision: which output should this packet go out of? It is not one single decision. It is thousands of micro-decisions that must be consistent with each other, otherwise the packet ends up in a loop or in a dead end.

Kurose and Ross therefore divide the network layer into two parts, and the distinction is absolutely essential for understanding the rest of the chapter:

The two planes

The data plane does the actual job: it moves a packet from the inputs of a router to the correct output. This happens in nanoseconds — billions of times per second on a large router. The operation is simple because the table is already in place.

The control plane builds that table. It determines which output is correct for each possible destination. This happens on a much slower timescale — seconds to minutes — and involves routers talking to each other to agree on the shape of the network.

The analogy that helps: the data plane is like a mail sorter who reads the address on the envelope and places it in the correct bin. The control plane is the route plan that determined which bin should go where — it is the back-room job that no customer sees, but without it the mail sorter would just toss things randomly.

Note the terminology. "Forwarding" is the data plane job (move the packet). "Routing" is the control plane job (find the route). In everyday speech we often use "routing" for both — but in this chapter, the distinction matters.

Two ways to organize the control plane

There are broadly two philosophies for how the control plane should be built:

1. Per-router control (traditional). Each router runs its own little routing algorithm, talks to its neighbors, and builds its own local forwarding table. No boss. Everyone is equal. This is how the Internet is built today, and it is what we focus on in this chapter.

2. Logically centralized control (Software Defined Networking, SDN). A central controller somewhere in the network computes all forwarding tables and pushes them out to all routers. The routers become "dumb" — they just do what the controller says. This is modern, and is used in large data centers, but is outside the scope of this chapter.

Check question
A packet arrives at a router, the router looks it up in its forwarding table and sends the packet out on port 3. Which plane was involved in this action?
Correct — the actual movement of the packet is a data plane operation. The control plane worked earlier (perhaps minutes earlier) to build the table, but at the moment the packet arrives, it is just a fast lookup.

02 · Routing protocols

How routers find good paths

The goal is easy to describe, but terribly difficult to solve: find a good path from sender to receiver through a network you do not have an overview of.

A routing protocol has one main goal: determine good routes (paths) from sending hosts to receiving hosts, through the network of routers. Let us unpack that sentence carefully, because every word means something.

A route (or path) is simply the sequence of routers a packet passes through, from the source host to the destination. If you send a packet from your laptop and it goes through routers R1 → R4 → R7 → R9 before reaching the target, that is the route.

But what does "good" mean? That depends on what you are optimizing for. Common interpretations are lowest cost (perhaps measured in hop count, perhaps in bandwidth, perhaps in peering agreements between ISPs), fastest (least delay), or least congested (avoiding links where there is queuing). Each routing protocol makes its own choices about what "good" means — and that is part of the reason routing is considered one of the classic "top 10" challenges in networking.

Routing is difficult because the network does not stand still. Links go down, come back up, become congested, gain new capacity. Routers come and go. A good algorithm must handle changes without everything stopping while it thinks.

Two axes of classification

Routing algorithms can be classified along two independent axes. If you understand these two, you understand the basic structure of everything we discuss going forward.

Axis 1 — Global or decentralized information?

Global means that all routers somehow know the entire network topology: which routers exist, which links they have between them, and what the cost of each link is. With full knowledge, each router can independently compute the best path to every destination. This is the idea behind link-state algorithms.

Decentralized means that no one has the full picture. Each router only knows the cost to its direct neighbors initially. Then routers talk to their neighbors, exchange what they know, and gradually everyone agrees on the best paths through an iterative process. This is the idea behind distance-vector algorithms.

Axis 2 — Static or dynamic?

Static routes change infrequently (perhaps when a network administrator manually configures something). Dynamic routes are updated continuously — either at regular intervals or in response to a link changing cost or disappearing. Modern networks almost always use dynamic algorithms, because the Internet changes too often to keep up manually.

Elaboration — outside the syllabus

What follows about link-state and distance-vector is not part of the syllabus (5.2.1 and 5.2.2 are explicitly excluded). It is included as useful background for understanding how routing algorithms actually work in practice.

Link-state: "I have the map"

Link-state algorithms are built on a simple but powerful idea: if every router has a complete map of the network, each one can compute the best paths entirely on its own, without having to ask anyone. The question then becomes: how does one obtain that map?

The solution is flooding. Each router creates a small message — a Link-State Advertisement (LSA) — that describes all the links it has and what they cost. Then it sends the LSA to all its neighbors. The neighbors forward it to their neighbors. And so on. After a short time, every router in the network has received LSAs from all others, and thus has knowledge of the entire topology.

Once a router has this complete map, it runs a shortest-path algorithm (typically Dijkstra's algorithm) locally to find the best path to every possible destination. The result is filled into the forwarding table, and the data plane is ready to move packets.

Advantages of link-state

Fast convergence: When something changes (a link goes down), the new information spreads quickly through the entire network, and all routers can update rapidly. Good scaling: Handles large, complex networks well. Adaptability: Suitable for networks that change frequently.

Distance-vector: "I trust my neighbors"

Distance-vector algorithms take an entirely different approach. Instead of everyone having the complete map, each router has only a distance vector — a table that says "to destination X, my best distance is Y, and I send the packet out via neighbor Z." The router gets no global overview; it gets only its own little list.

Here is how it works in practice:

Step 1 — Information sharing. Each router sends its own distance vector to its direct neighbors. "This is what the world looks like from my vantage point," it says.

Step 2 — Updating. When a router receives a neighbor's vector, it compares: "Hmm, neighbor B says it can reach destination X with cost 5. To reach neighbor B costs me 2. So if I send via B, I can reach X with cost 7. Is that better than what I had?" If yes, it updates its own table.

Step 3 — Convergence. Every time a router's table changes, it sends out its new vector to its neighbors, who might update their tables, who might send out new vectors, etc. After a while, everything stabilizes, and all routers have consistent and (hopefully) optimal tables.

The analogy. Link-state is like giving everyone a GPS map of the entire city. Distance-vector is like asking your neighbors: "How far is it to the store from here?" — and they ask their neighbors — until you have all agreed on the shortest route.

Check question
A new router-neighbor pair discovers that the link between them has just gotten twice the delay. Which algorithm type will typically react fastest to this change?
Correct — link-state spreads changes via flooding, so all routers learn about it almost immediately and can recalculate their tables. Distance-vector can take longer because information must "ripple" through the network one neighbor at a time.

03 · Internet Control Message Protocol

ICMP: when the network must talk to itself

Routing protocols determine where packets should go. But what happens when something goes wrong along the way? That is where ICMP comes in.

Imagine you send a packet to an IP address that does not exist. What happens? Someone must tell you — otherwise you sit waiting forever for a reply that never comes. Or: a packet has lived too long in the network (perhaps it went in a loop) and must be discarded. Who notifies the sender? The answer to both questions is ICMP — Internet Control Message Protocol.

ICMP is the network's signaling language. It is used by hosts and routers to exchange network-level information with each other. The two most important categories of messages are:

Error reporting. When something goes wrong — destination host unreachable, network unreachable, port closed, protocol not available — a router or host sends an ICMP message back to the sender explaining what went wrong.

Echo request/reply. This is what the ping command uses. You send an "are you there?" message, and if the receiver is alive, it sends back a "yes!"

Where does ICMP fit in the stack?

Here is a detail that often confuses: ICMP is considered part of the network layer, but ICMP messages are transported inside IP datagrams. So strictly speaking, ICMP sits "on top of" IP, even though both are considered part of the network layer. Think of ICMP as a small helper protocol that piggybacks on IP to get where it needs to go.

An ICMP message contains three main fields:

  • Type — which category of message this is (e.g. 3 = "destination unreachable", 8 = "echo request")
  • Code — a subcategory that provides more detail (e.g. type 3 + code 1 = "host unreachable")
  • The first 8 bytes of the IP datagram that caused the error — so the sender knows which packet the ICMP message refers to

The most important ICMP types

04 · Traceroute

Traceroute: an ingenious hack

How can you find out which routers your packets pass through on the way to a destination — without having access to any of them? The answer is one of the networking world's finest tricks.

The command traceroute (and variants like tracert on Windows, mtr, etc.) shows you a list of all routers a packet passes on the way to a target. It is incredibly useful when troubleshooting — but how on earth does it do it? You cannot ask each router along the way for its name. They do not even know you are trying to reach them.

The trick exploits a feature that was actually designed to prevent packets from living forever: the TTL field (Time To Live) in the IP header. TTL is a number that is decremented by 1 each time a packet passes through a router. When TTL hits 0, the router discards the packet and sends an ICMP message back to the sender: "type 11, code 0 — TTL expired." And — this is the key — that message contains the router's own IP address.

Can you see where this is going?

The traceroute trick

If you send a packet with TTL = 1, the first router will decrement it to 0 and return a TTL-expired message. You learn the first router's address.

Then send a packet with TTL = 2. It survives the first router, but is dropped by the second, which returns a TTL-expired message. You learn the second router's address.

Continue with TTL = 3, 4, 5, ... and you have the entire path.

That is how simple it is, and how beautiful. Traceroute exploits the TTL mechanism to force each router on the path to "reveal" itself, one by one.

How it works step by step

The source machine sends out sets of UDP segments (usually three per TTL value, to be able to measure RTT variation). Each packet in a set has the same TTL.

  • Set 1 has TTL = 1 → dies at router 1 → router 1 sends "TTL expired"
  • Set 2 has TTL = 2 → dies at router 2 → router 2 sends "TTL expired"
  • Set 3 has TTL = 3 → dies at router 3 → router 3 sends "TTL expired"
  • ... and so on

When the ICMP message comes back, the sender records the round-trip time (RTT) and the router's IP. It displays them progressively on your screen — that is what you see while traceroute is running.

When does it stop?

Two things can happen that terminate traceroute:

1. The UDP segment eventually actually reaches the destination (when TTL is large enough). Now there is no router dropping it — but the destination most likely has no service listening on the UDP port traceroute used (it deliberately chooses an unlikely port). So the destination responds with an ICMP "destination port unreachable" message (type 3, code 3).

2. The source sees this message and understands: aha, we have reached the target. It stops.

The beauty is that traceroute never actually "asked for" the information. It just exploited two entirely normal ICMP mechanisms — TTL expired and port unreachable — to force a result that no one had intended it to get. It is the hacker spirit at its finest.

Why does traceroute send UDP segments to an unlikely high port number on the destination?
Exactly right. Traceroute needs a clear "end" signal from the destination. By sending to a port no one listens on, it guarantees the destination responds with ICMP "port unreachable" (type 3, code 3) — which tells traceroute that the next step is not an intermediate router, but the target itself.

05 · Conclusion

And finally: network management

Routing algorithms and ICMP are the building blocks. But someone must put it all together, monitor it, and fix it when things break.

Not part of the syllabus. Network management is not part of the syllabus for chapter 5, but provides useful context.

A modern autonomous system — the technical term for "a network operated by one organization," for example an ISP, a university, or a large enterprise — consists of thousands of interacting hardware and software components. Keeping something this complex in order requires its own discipline: network management.

The classic definition from Saydam & Magedanz (1996) captures the breadth well: network management encompasses the deployment, integration, and coordination of hardware, software, and human elements to monitor, test, configure, analyze, evaluate, and control the network and its resources — so that performance and quality-of-service requirements are met at a reasonable cost.

Translated into everyday language: someone must make sure the lights are on, nothing is on fire, and the users are not calling to complain. Other complex systems have the same type of challenge — a passenger aircraft, a nuclear power plant, a large factory — all must be continuously monitored and controlled to avoid collapse.

What to take away from this chapter

The network layer has two faces: the data plane that rapidly moves packets according to pre-built tables, and the control plane that builds those tables by having routers talk to each other via routing protocols. Link-state algorithms give everyone the full map; distance-vector algorithms let each router learn from its neighbors. And when something goes wrong, or when we simply want to trace the path a packet took, ICMP serves as the network's internal signaling language — quiet and discreet, but absolutely essential.
