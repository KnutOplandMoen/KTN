01 · Introduction

What is network security?

Four properties that a secure communication system must satisfy — and what happens when it does not.

When Alice sends a message to Bob over the Internet, it passes through dozens of routers, switches, and links that neither of them controls. Anyone along the way — a curious network administrator, a hacker on an open WiFi, a state intelligence agency — can in principle listen, alter, or block the message. Network security is about making it impossible, or at least computationally infeasible, for a third party to misuse the communication.

A secure communication system must satisfy four fundamental properties:

Confidentiality

Only the sender and intended receiver should be able to understand the message content. The sender encrypts, the receiver decrypts. Everyone else sees only unintelligible noise.

Authentication

Sender and receiver should be able to confirm each other's identity. When you log into your online bank, the bank wants to know it is really you — and you want to know you are really talking to the bank, not a fake copy.

Message integrity

The content of the message must not be alterable en route — neither by an attacker nor by random errors — without the receiver detecting it.

Availability

Services must actually be available to legitimate users. A Denial-of-Service attack (DoS) attempts precisely to break this property by flooding a server with so much traffic that it cannot serve genuine requests.

Alice, Bob, and Trudy

In the security world, we use three recurring characters: Alice and Bob want to communicate securely with each other. Trudy (from "intruder") is the attacker who sits between them and tries to sabotage. Trudy can do surprisingly much:

Who are Alice and Bob in practice? They could be a browser and an online bank, two DNS servers exchanging lookups, two BGP routers sharing routing tables, or simply two people sending email. Trudy can be anyone with access to the network between them.

What can an attacker do?

  • Eavesdrop: passively intercept messages without anyone noticing.
  • Insert messages: actively inject fake packets into an ongoing connection.
  • Impersonate others (spoofing): forge the source address of a packet so it appears to come from someone else.
  • Hijack a connection: take over an ongoing TCP connection by removing one party and stepping in their place.
  • Denial of Service (DoS): flood a server with requests so it cannot serve legitimate users.
02 · Cryptography

Symmetric key cryptography

When sender and receiver share one and the same secret key.

Cryptography is the toolbox that makes the four security properties possible. The basic setup is always the same: a plaintext m is passed through an encryption algorithm together with a key KA, and the result is a ciphertext that is unreadable to anyone who does not have the correct decryption key KB. The receiver uses KB to recover the plaintext: m = KB(KA(m)).

In symmetric key cryptography, the encryption and decryption key are the same: KA = KB = KS. Both parties must therefore know the same secret. This raises an immediate question: how do they agree on the key in the first place? But let us first look at the mechanisms themselves.

Substitution cipher: the simplest example

The most basic encryption scheme is a monoalphabetic substitution cipher: replace each letter with another, consistently throughout the entire message. For example: a→m, b→n, c→b, d→v, etc. The key is the entire substitution table — a permutation of 26 letters.

The problem is that such ciphers are easy to break with statistical analysis. The letter "e" is the most common in English text, so the letter that appears most frequently in the ciphertext is probably "e". With a few hundred characters of ciphertext, one can typically crack the entire key. A polyalphabetic cipher — where one rotates among several substitution tables — is better, but still vulnerable.

Breaking encryption

An attacker can try to break an encryption in three ways:

Attack typeWhat Trudy hasStrategy
Ciphertext-only Only the ciphertext Brute force (try all keys) or statistical analysis
Known-plaintext Some plaintext-ciphertext pairs Use the pairs to derive the key
Chosen-plaintext Can choose plaintext and see the resulting ciphertext Choose strategic plaintext to uncover the key structure

DES — Data Encryption Standard

DES was the U.S. standard for symmetric encryption from 1977 (FIPS PUB 46). It uses a 56-bit key and operates on 64-bit blocks of plaintext. DES is a block cipher: the plaintext is divided into fixed-size blocks that are encrypted individually, and the blocks are chained together via cipher block chaining (CBC) to prevent identical plaintext blocks from producing identical ciphertext.

How secure is DES? Not secure enough. In the DES Challenge, a 56-bit key was cracked by brute force in less than a day. The solution was 3DES: encrypt three times with three different keys, effectively providing a 168-bit key strength.

AES — Advanced Encryption Standard

AES replaced DES in 2001 as the NIST standard. It operates on 128-bit blocks and supports key lengths of 128, 192, or 256 bits. To put it in perspective: if brute-forcing DES takes one second, brute-forcing AES with a 128-bit key would take approximately 149 trillion years. AES is today the dominant symmetric algorithm — it is used in TLS, WiFi (WPA2/WPA3), IPsec, and much more.

Symmetric cryptography is fast — it is used to encrypt the actual data. But it has one major problem: how do Alice and Bob exchange the key securely? The answer comes in the next section.
03 · Public key

Public key cryptography

A radical idea: publish one key for the entire world — and keep the other one strictly private.

Symmetric cryptography has a fundamental problem: Alice and Bob must agree on a shared secret key in advance. If they have never met physically, how do they do so without Trudy intercepting the key? Public key cryptography — proposed independently by Diffie and Hellman (1976) and by the RSA group (Rivest, Shamir, Adleman, 1978) — solves this in an elegant way.

The idea is simple yet revolutionary: Bob has two keys. One — the public key KB+ — he publishes for the entire world. The other — the private key KB− — he keeps strictly to himself. The system has two crucial properties:

  1. If you encrypt with the public key, only the private key can decrypt: KB−(KB+(m)) = m
  2. Given the public key, it is computationally infeasible to find the private key.

In practice, the two are combined: public key cryptography is used to exchange a shared symmetric session key, and then the fast symmetric algorithm (e.g., AES) is used to encrypt the actual data stream. This is the pattern we will see again and again — in secure email, in TLS, and in IPsec.

Public key cryptography revolutionized 2,000 years of cryptographic history. Before 1976, only symmetric methods existed, and key exchange was always the hardest problem.

04 · Integrity

Message integrity and digital signatures

How to prove that a message has not been altered en route — and that it really was Bob who sent it.

Encryption gives us confidentiality, but that is not enough. Even if Trudy cannot read the message, she may be able to change some bits in the ciphertext without knowing what she is changing. Or she can send an entirely new message and pretend it comes from Bob. We need two more things: message integrity (the content is unaltered) and authentication (the sender is who they claim to be).

MAC — symmetric integrity and sender authentication

When Alice and Bob already share a secret key KS, they can attach a Message Authentication Code (MAC) to the message: a short value that only someone who knows KS could have computed correctly. The receiver computes the same MAC from the message and the key; if it does not match, the message was altered or forged.

HMAC (Hash-based Message Authentication Code) is the usual construction in practice: a cryptographic hash (e.g. SHA-256) is combined with the secret key in a standardized way (RFC 2104). Simply hashing the key and message together with naive patterns (e.g. H(KS || m)) is not safe — length-extension attacks and other weaknesses motivated the precise HMAC definition.

MAC versus digital signature

MAC: requires a shared secret key; provides integrity and that the message must come from someone who knows the key (often two known parties, e.g. in TLS or WiFi).

Digital signature: uses a private/public key pair; the receiver does not need a shared secret with the sender, and you can get non-repudiation toward a third party.

Digital signatures

A digital signature is the cryptographic version of a handwritten signature, but much stronger. Bob signs a message m by encrypting it (or a hash of it) with his private key KB−. The result is the signature KB−(m).

When Alice receives the message and the signature, she applies Bob's public key KB+ to the signature. If KB+(KB−(m)) = m, she knows two things:

  1. Bob signed the message — because only Bob has the private key that could produce that signature.
  2. The message is unaltered — because any change to m would cause the verification to fail.

In addition, we get non-repudiation: Alice can show the message and signature to a third party (e.g., a court) and prove that Bob must have signed, because only Bob's private key could have produced that signature.

Message digest and hash functions

Public-key-encrypting an entire long message is computationally expensive. The solution is to use a hash function H that creates a short "fingerprint" of the message — a message digest H(m) of fixed length. Instead of signing all of m, Bob signs only H(m). Alice verifies by: (1) computing H(m) herself from the received message, (2) decrypting the signature with Bob's public key, and (3) checking that the two hashes are equal.

A cryptographic hash function has three important properties:

  • Many-to-one: different messages can in principle produce the same hash (but it should be extremely unlikely).
  • Fixed length: regardless of how long the message is, the hash produces a fixed-size output.
  • One-way function: given a hash value x, it is computationally infeasible to find a message m such that H(m) = x.
Why not Internet checksum?

TCP already uses a checksum for error detection — why can't we use that? Because the Internet checksum is too simple. It is easy to find two different messages that produce exactly the same checksum. For example, "IOU 100.99 BOB" and "IOU 900.19 BOB" produce identical 16-bit checksums. A cryptographic hash function (such as SHA-256) makes this practically impossible.

Certificate Authorities (CA)

The entire system relies on Alice having Bob's genuine public key. But what if Trudy pretends to be Bob and publishes her own public key under Bob's name? Then Trudy can sign messages that appear to come from Bob.

The solution is a Certification Authority (CA) — a trusted third party that binds a public key to an identity. Bob gives his key to a CA, proves who he is, and the CA creates a certificate: a document containing Bob's identity and public key, digitally signed by the CA with the CA's private key.

When Alice needs Bob's public key, she fetches the certificate and verifies the CA's signature using the CA's public key. But wait — where does the CA's public key come from? It typically comes pre-installed in your browser or operating system. That is why the browser can verify that an SSL certificate from a website is genuine — it has the CA's root key built in from the factory.
