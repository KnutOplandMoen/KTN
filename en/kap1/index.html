Computer Networks and the Internet

What exactly is the Internet? How does data travel from A to B, what does it cost in time — and why is everything organized in layers?

The big picture

Before you dive into the details — here is the map of the terrain.

When you open Netflix, your laptop sends small chunks of data — packets — that travel through perhaps ten to fifteen routers before reaching a Netflix server somewhere else in the world. The server sends video packets back, and they find their way through an entirely different set of routers. All of this happens in a few tens of milliseconds, governed by a set of rules called protocols. Chapter 1 is about understanding this machinery at a high level: what is the Internet made of, how does data move, and why is everything organized the way it is?

The analogy you need

Think of the Internet as a postal system. Your house and mine are end systems (hosts). The post offices are routers that sort and forward. The letters are packets — data divided into manageable chunks with an address label (header) at the front. And the shared rules for how to address, stamp, and deliver mail are protocols. You drop the letter in the mailbox (the socket) and trust that the system delivers — without knowing which route it takes.

A crucial insight is that the Internet uses packet switching, not circuit switching. This means that a dedicated connection is never set up between you and the server — your packets share the links with everyone else, and capacity is allocated dynamically. This is why the Internet can serve billions of users without reserving a line for every single conversation. The price is that delays can vary and packets can be lost — but the gain in efficiency is enormous.

To manage all this complexity, network communication is organized into five layers, where each layer has one job and does not concern itself with the details of the layers below. This layering is the very foundation upon which the rest of the course is built:

Why does this matter?

Everything you learn in the rest of the course — HTTP, TCP, IP, Ethernet — is one layer in this stack. Once you understand the layering, you also understand why the course is structured the way it is: we start at the top (applications you know) and work our way down to the bits on the wire. Chapter 1 gives you the entire map; the remaining chapters zoom in on each layer.

  1. What is a network protocol, and what three things does it define?

    Answer: A protocol is a shared set of rules that defines (1) the format of messages exchanged, (2) the order in which they are sent and received, and (3) the actions taken on send and receive.

    Why it matters: Without an agreed protocol, bits are just voltages or light pulses with no meaning. Two devices without a common protocol cannot interpret each other. Everything from HTTP to IP to Ethernet is ultimately such an agreement.

  2. Compare packet switching and circuit switching. Why did packet switching win?

    Answer: Packet switching shares link capacity dynamically by sending data as independent packets. Circuit switching reserves a dedicated circuit (frequency band or time slot) end-to-end before communication starts.

    Why packet switching won: Statistical multiplexing. Users are rarely active at the same time. Packet switching uses idle periods; circuit switching leaves reserved capacity unused. Example: on a 1 Mbps link with users active 10% of the time, TDM supports about 10 users, packet switching about 35, with under 0.04% overload probability. The tradeoff is no delay or loss guarantees with packet switching.

  3. Explain store-and-forward. What is total transmission delay for one packet over N links (transmission only)?

    Answer: A router must receive the entire packet before it can forward it. For one packet of L bits on a link of rate R bps, transmission time is L/R. Over N links (N−1 routers), total transmission delay is N × L/R.

    Why: The router needs the full header and payload before forwarding reliably. Each hop repeats the L/R cost, so smaller packets finish faster, all else equal.

  4. What is the difference between transmission delay and propagation delay?

    Answer: Transmission delay (L/R) is the time to push all bits onto the link — depends on packet size and link rate. Propagation delay (d/v) is the time for bits to travel through the medium — depends on distance and signal speed (~2×10⁸ m/s in fiber/copper).

    Why people confuse them: Both are time, but they are governed by different physics. Caravan analogy: time at the toll booth ≈ transmission; time on the road ≈ propagation. You can speed up the booth without shortening the road, and vice versa.

  5. Compute transmission delay for a 1500-byte packet on a 100 Mbps link. What is it on 1 Gbps?

    Answer: 1500 bytes = 12,000 bits.
    At 100 Mbps: d_trans = 12 000 / (100×10⁶) = 120 µs.
    At 1 Gbps: d_trans = 12 000 / 10⁹ = 12 µs.

    Why: d_trans scales with 1/R. Convert bytes to bits (×8) and express R in bps — the most common mistake is forgetting that conversion.

  6. What does traffic intensity La/R tell you, and what happens as it approaches 1?

    Answer: La/R is (average packet length × arrival rate) / link rate — the fraction of capacity used on average. As it approaches 1, average queueing delay grows toward infinity. Above 1, arrivals exceed service rate and packets are dropped when buffers overflow.

    Why it “explodes” near 1: Arrivals are bursty; random bunching fills the queue faster than it drains. Networks are designed to keep intensity well below 1.

  7. What is a bottleneck link, and where is it usually found?

    Answer: The bottleneck is the lowest-capacity link on an end-to-end path; it limits throughput.

    Where: Often in the access network (last mile — DSL, mobile, home WiFi) or at the server. The Internet core uses very fast fiber, so it is rarely the bottleneck. That is why 1 Gbps fiber at home does not always feel 10× faster than 100 Mbps.

  8. Describe the five layers of the Internet protocol stack and what each is responsible for.

    Answer (top-down):

    5 — Application: messages between processes (HTTP, SMTP, DNS).
    4 — Transport: end-to-end delivery between processes, multiplexing (TCP, UDP).
    3 — Network: routing datagrams source to destination (IP, ICMP).
    2 — Link: frame transfer between adjacent nodes (Ethernet, WiFi).
    1 — Physical: bits on a medium (voltages, light, radio).

    Why layering: Each layer only uses the layer below and only offers services to the layer above, so layers can be swapped independently (e.g., WiFi vs Ethernet without TCP caring).

  9. What is encapsulation, and why does it matter?

    Answer: Each layer wraps data from the layer above with its own header (and sometimes a trailer). An application message becomes a transport segment (TCP/UDP header), then a network datagram (IP header), then a link-layer frame (MAC header, FCS trailer).

    Why it matters: Each layer sees only its own header; the payload is opaque “data.” On the receiver, headers are stripped in reverse order, so layers stay independent and composable.

  10. How does the Internet five-layer model differ from the seven-layer OSI model?

    Answer: OSI adds two layers between application and transport: presentation (compression, encryption, format conversion) and session (session control, synchronization). The Internet does not split these out; applications or libraries handle them.

    Why it matters: OSI is academic vocabulary (“layer 7” = application). Real Internet stacks use five layers; TLS and similar run as libraries on top of the transport/application boundary.

  11. What is the role of the socket interface?

    Answer: The socket is the API between an application and the transport layer — the “door” apps send and receive through. It lets the app request delivery to a destination without knowing routers, IP details, or link behavior.

    Why: Same idea as the postal analogy: you drop the letter in the box and trust the system below.

  12. How is the ISP hierarchy organized? What is an IXP?

    Answer: Access ISPs connect end systems; regional ISPs interconnect access ISPs; Tier-1 ISPs (e.g. Lumen, NTT) have global reach and peer with each other without settlement.

    An IXP (Internet Exchange Point) is a physical location where many ISPs exchange traffic by peering, reducing transit cost and often RTT.

  13. Explain three common denial-of-service (DoS) attack patterns.

    Answer: (1) Vulnerability attack — crafted messages crash or hang the service. (2) Bandwidth flooding — saturate the link; often needs a botnet (DDoS). (3) Connection flooding — many half-open TCP connections (e.g. SYN flood) exhaust server state.

    Why different: Each targets software robustness, link capacity, or connection tables — defenses differ (patching, upstream filtering, rate limits).

  14. What is IP spoofing, and why is it dangerous?

    Answer: Sending packets with a forged source IP so the receiver trusts the wrong identity.

    Why dangerous: IP does not authenticate senders. Used in reflection DDoS (replies hit the victim) and to bypass IP-only access controls.

  15. What does ping measure, and what does traceroute reveal that ping does not?

    Answer: ping sends an ICMP echo request and measures RTT to a host — reachability and total delay.

    traceroute sends packets with increasing TTL; each router returns ICMP time exceeded, giving a hop-by-hop path with delay per hop.

    Use: Ping gives one number; traceroute shows where delay appears. Both use ICMP and may show * * * when firewalls block.
