The big picture

Before you dive into the details — here is the map of the terrain.

When you open Netflix, your laptop sends small chunks of data — packets — that travel through perhaps ten to fifteen routers before reaching a Netflix server somewhere else in the world. The server sends video packets back, and they find their way through an entirely different set of routers. All of this happens in a few tens of milliseconds, governed by a set of rules called protocols. Chapter 1 is about understanding this machinery at a high level: what is the Internet made of, how does data move, and why is everything organized the way it is?

The analogy you need

Think of the Internet as a postal system. Your house and mine are end systems (hosts). The post offices are routers that sort and forward. The letters are packets — data divided into manageable chunks with an address label (header) at the front. And the shared rules for how to address, stamp, and deliver mail are protocols. You drop the letter in the mailbox (the socket) and trust that the system delivers — without knowing which route it takes.

A crucial insight is that the Internet uses packet switching, not circuit switching. This means that a dedicated connection is never set up between you and the server — your packets share the links with everyone else, and capacity is allocated dynamically. This is why the Internet can serve billions of users without reserving a line for every single conversation. The price is that delays can vary and packets can be lost — but the gain in efficiency is enormous.

Why does this matter?

Everything you learn in the rest of the course — HTTP, TCP, IP, Ethernet — is one layer in this stack. Once you understand the layering, you also understand why the course is structured the way it is: we start at the top (applications you know) and work our way down to the bits on the wire. Chapter 1 gives you the entire map; the remaining chapters zoom in on each layer.

Test yourself

Check whether you have understood the most important concepts from this chapter.

Question 1
What is the key difference between packet switching and circuit switching?
In packet switching, capacity is shared dynamically through statistical multiplexing — packets from different users are interleaved and share the links. In circuit switching, a fixed portion of capacity (frequency band or time slot) is reserved for the entire call, even when the user is idle. Packet switching is more efficient but provides no guarantees for delay or loss.
Question 2
What defines a network protocol?
A protocol is a set of rules that defines the format, order, and actions associated with message exchange between two or more communicating entities. Without protocols, machines would send bits with no shared understanding of what they mean.
Question 3
Which of the four delay components is fundamentally unpredictable and depends on network traffic?
Queuing delay is the only one of the four components that is fundamentally unpredictable. It depends on how many other packets are already waiting in the router queue — something that varies with network traffic. The other three are either constant (processing, propagation) or determined by known values (transmission = L/R).
