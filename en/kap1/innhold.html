01 · Introduction

What is the Internet?

Two complementary ways of thinking about the same network.

The Internet is something most people use for hundreds of minutes every day, yet very few can give a precise definition of. There are two ways to answer the question "what is the Internet?" — and both are equally valid. The first is the nuts-and-bolts description: what does it physically consist of? The second is the service description: what does it offer to applications that use it?

The nuts-and-bolts description

The Internet is a network of billions of end systems (also called hosts) connected via communication links and packet switches. End systems range from laptops and smartphones to servers, IoT sensors, and smart refrigerators. The links — fiber, copper cable, radio — bind them together, and packet switches (primarily routers and link-layer switches) forward data between them.

When an end system needs to send data, the data is divided into packets — small chunks with a header at the front that tells the network where they should go. Each packet travels independently through the network, hopping from switch to switch, and is reassembled at the receiver. End systems connect to the Internet through ISPs (Internet Service Providers), which in turn are connected in a hierarchy. The entire system is driven by protocols — rules for how devices communicate — where TCP and IP are the two most important.

What is a protocol?

A protocol defines the format and order of messages exchanged between two or more communicating entities, as well as the actions taken when a message is sent or received. Without protocols, two machines would send bits to each other with no shared understanding of what they mean.

The service description

From an application perspective, the Internet is an infrastructure that provides services to distributed applications. Email, the web, video streaming, online gaming, social media — all run on top of the Internet. The applications run on end systems, not inside the network, and they communicate with each other using a socket interface. The socket is the "door" between the application and the transport layer — it allows the application to request that data be delivered to another machine, without needing to know anything about routers and links along the way.

Analogy

Think of the Internet as a postal system. You (the application) drop a letter in the mailbox (the socket). The postal system (the network) takes care of the rest — sorting, transporting, delivering. You don't need to know which route the letter takes or which trucks are used. You trust that there is a set of rules (protocols) that ensures the letter arrives.

02 · The edge

The network edge

End systems, access networks, and physical media — where you and the applications live.

The network edge is where the real action happens. This is where your laptop runs a browser, your smartphone streams video, and a server responds to HTTP requests. All of these are end systems — they sit at the edge of the network and do the useful work. Routers and switches inside the network do nothing the application cares about; they simply forward packets.

Access networks — the last mile

The access network is the link between your end system and the first router (edge router) in the network. It is often the narrowest and most expensive part of the entire connection, and a handful of technologies are used:

Physical media

Regardless of the technology, bits must ultimately travel over a physical medium. We distinguish between guided media (the signal follows a physical conductor) and unguided media (the signal propagates freely):

Guided media

Twisted-pair cable — inexpensive, used in Ethernet and DSL, up to 10 Gbps over short distances. Coaxial cable — used in cable TV networks, shared medium, good bandwidth. Fiber-optic cable — light pulses in glass, enormous bandwidth, low attenuation, immune to electromagnetic interference.

Unguided media

Terrestrial radio (WiFi, Bluetooth) — short range, affected by walls and interference. Microwave (4G/5G) — longer range, widely used in mobile networks. Satellite — global coverage possible, but latency is a challenge (GEO: ~280 ms one-way, ~560 ms RTT).

Fiber-optic cable is the only medium that is not degraded by electromagnetic noise. That is why it is the preferred medium for long distances and high speeds — undersea cables between continents are almost exclusively fiber.
03 · The network core

The network core

Packet switching vs. circuit switching — two fundamentally different ways to share a network.

The core of the Internet is a mesh of routers connected by high-speed fiber links. No end systems reside here — only switches that forward packets. But how they forward follows one of two principles that are worth understanding thoroughly, because they define everything that happens above.

Packet switching

The Internet uses packet switching. Data is divided into packets that are sent independently through the network. Each router that receives a packet stores it completely (store), checks the destination, and forwards it to the next router (forward). This is called store-and-forward: the entire packet must be received before it can be forwarded.

Store-and-forward — the consequence

If a packet is L bits long and the link speed is R bits/second, it takes L/R seconds to transmit the packet onto the link. And the router cannot begin forwarding it until it has received everything — that is, after another L/R seconds. For a single packet over N links, the total delay is N × L/R.

Circuit switching

The traditional telephone network uses circuit switching. Before a call starts, a dedicated connection (circuit) is set up all the way from sender to receiver. The resources (bandwidth, time slot, frequency band) are reserved and guaranteed to be available for the entire call — but they are also blocked for everyone else, even when you are silent.

Resources in a circuit can be divided in two ways:

FDM (Frequency Division)

The frequency band is divided into narrow bands. Each call gets its own frequency band. Everyone transmits simultaneously, but on different frequencies.

TDM (Time Division)

Time is divided into frames with fixed time slots. Each call gets its time slot in each frame. Everyone shares the same frequency, but transmits in turn.

Why did packet switching win?

The answer is statistical multiplexing. Users are rarely active all the time — you alternate between reading a page, clicking a link, waiting. With packet switching, the network's capacity is shared dynamically: when you are idle, others use the capacity. With circuit switching, the reserved capacity would sit unused. The result is that packet switching can serve far more users than circuit switching for the same amount of network resources.

Example

A link with 1 Mbps capacity. Each user needs 100 kbps when active, but is only active 10% of the time. With circuit switching (TDM): 10 users max. With packet switching: 35 users can share the link, and the probability that more than 10 are active simultaneously is under 0.04%. Packet switching wins with 3.5× more users — for a very small loss of guarantee.

A network of networks

The Internet is not a single network, but a network of networks. Millions of access ISPs (Telenor, Telia, your local fiber provider) connect end systems. These access ISPs are in turn connected to regional ISPs, which connect up to a small number of Tier 1 ISPs (e.g., Lumen, NTT, Telia Carrier) that have global reach. In addition, IXPs (Internet Exchange Points) exist where ISPs meet and exchange traffic directly, and large content providers like Google and Netflix build their own private networks to reach users faster.

04 · Delay

Delay, loss, and throughput

The four components that together make up the delay — and why packets sometimes disappear.

When a packet travels from A to B, it takes time. This delay is not one thing — it is the sum of four different components, each with its own cause and behavior. Understanding these four is the key to understanding why the network sometimes feels slow.

The four delay components

1. Processing delay (d_proc)

The time the router spends examining the packet's header, checking for bit errors, and determining which output link the packet should go to. Typically microseconds — negligible in most contexts.

2. Queuing delay (d_queue)

The time the packet waits in the router queue before it gets to go out on the link. Depends on how many other packets are already waiting. Can range from zero (no queue) to milliseconds (long queue), and is the only one of the four that is fundamentally unpredictable.

3. Transmission delay (d_trans)

The time it takes to push all the bits onto the link. Depends on the packet length L and the link speed R: d_trans = L / R. A 1500-byte packet on a 1 Gbps link: 12 µs. The same packet on a 1 Mbps link: 12 ms — a thousand times more.

4. Propagation delay (d_prop)

The time the signal takes to physically travel through the medium — in copper or fiber at approximately 2 × 10⁸ m/s. d_prop = distance / speed. Norway to the USA (~6000 km): ~30 ms. Same room: negligible. This delay cannot be reduced without moving the machines closer together.

The total nodal delay for a single router is therefore:

d_nodal = d_proc + d_queue + d_trans + d_prop
Transmission delay and propagation delay are the two most commonly confused. Transmission is about how fast you push bits onto the link (depends on link speed). Propagation is about how long the bits take to travel through the medium (depends on physical distance). They are two entirely different things.

Queuing delay and traffic intensity

Queuing delay is the most interesting of the four, because it is the only one that explodes under load. We measure the load with traffic intensity:

Traffic intensity = (L × a) / R

Where a is the average number of packets arriving per second. When the traffic intensity approaches 1, the queuing delay grows toward infinity. Above 1, the queue grows faster than it drains — packets are dropped because the buffer is full. This is packet loss.

End-to-end delay

Over an entire route with N links (and N−1 routers), the delays are summed. If all links are identical:

d_end-to-end = N × (d_proc + d_queue + d_trans + d_prop)

Throughput

Throughput is the effective rate at which data is delivered from sender to receiver, measured in bits per second. For a route with multiple links, the throughput is limited by the narrowest link — the bottleneck link. If you have a 1 Gbps fiber at home but the server sends over a 10 Mbps link, your throughput is 10 Mbps.

In practice, the bottleneck is most often in the access network (last mile) or at the server itself, not in the network core where the links are enormously fast.

Measuring delay in practice: traceroute and ping

How do you actually know what the delay is on a given route? Two classic tools let you find out:

traceroute (tracert on Windows)

Sends series of special packets that "expire" at each router along the route to a destination. Each router the packet passes through sends an error message back, and traceroute measures the round-trip time (RTT) to each individual router. The result is a list of all routers along the path and the delay to each of them — so you can see where the delay occurs.

ping

Sends a simple ICMP Echo Request packet to a destination and measures the time it takes to receive a reply (RTT). It is the quickest way to check whether a host is reachable and how long the delay is. ping also shows whether packets are lost, which indicates network problems.

Both tools use the ICMP protocol (Internet Control Message Protocol) at the network layer. Some routers and firewalls block ICMP, so traceroute sometimes shows "* * *" for certain hops.

05 · Protocol layers

Protocol layers and service models

Why all network communication is organized in a stack of layers — and what each layer does.

A computer network is enormously complex: it must handle everything from electromagnetic signals in a cable to video streaming in a browser. To tame this complexity, we divide the functionality into layers, where each layer offers a service to the layer above and uses the services of the layer below. It is like a postal system where you never talk to the driver — you drop the letter in the mailbox, and each intermediary takes care of its part.

The five layers of the Internet

#LayerProtocols (examples)Responsibility
5Application layerHTTP, SMTP, DNS, FTPMessages between applications
4Transport layerTCP, UDPEnd-to-end data delivery between processes
3Network layerIP, ICMPRouting of packets (datagrams) from source to destination
2Link layerEthernet, WiFi (802.11)Transferring frames between neighboring nodes
1Physical layer—Bits over a physical medium (voltages, light pulses, radio waves)

The OSI model — seven layers

The alternative OSI reference model (Open Systems Interconnection) has seven layers instead of five. The two extra layers — the presentation layer and the session layer — sit between the application and transport layers. In the Internet, these functions (data compression, encryption, session management) are left to the application itself, and the layers do not exist as separate entities. OSI is important as a historical reference and in academic contexts, but in practice it is the five-layer model that describes the real Internet.

Encapsulation — layers wrap inside layers

The most elegant consequence of layering is encapsulation. When the application sends a message, the transport layer wraps it in a segment by adding its own header. The network layer wraps the segment in a datagram with an IP header. The link layer wraps the datagram in a frame with its header (and trailer). Each layer sees only its own header — what is inside is "just data" from the layer's perspective.

Why is this so powerful?

Encapsulation means that each layer is independent. You can swap Ethernet for WiFi at the link layer without TCP caring. You can add encryption at the application layer without IP knowing about it. The layers are like Lego bricks — they can be freely combined, as long as the interface between them is respected.

06 · Security threats

Networks under attack

The Internet was not designed with security in mind — and attackers have exploited this from the start.

The early designers of the Internet focused on robustness and openness, not security. The result is that the attack surfaces are numerous and serious. Here are the most important categories you should know about already — we go deeper in Chapter 8.

Malware

Malicious software that infects end systems. Viruses spread by inserting themselves into files the user actively opens. Worms spread by themselves without user interaction — they exploit vulnerabilities in network services and can infect thousands of machines in minutes. Once a machine is infected, it can become part of a botnet — a network of compromised machines that are remotely controlled.

Denial-of-service attacks (DoS / DDoS)

The goal is to make a service unavailable to legitimate users. In a DoS attack, the attacker floods the target with traffic, so the server or network is too busy to serve real requests. In a DDoS attack (Distributed DoS), the traffic comes from thousands of machines in a botnet — much harder to stop because the attack traffic looks like normal traffic from many different sources.

Three attack patterns for DoS

Vulnerability exploitation: Send specially crafted messages that crash the service. Bandwidth flooding: Send a massive volume of packets that fill up the link's capacity. Connection flooding: Open an enormous number of half-open TCP connections that exhaust the server's resources (SYN flooding).

Packet sniffing

A passive receiver that listens to traffic passing by — especially dangerous on shared media like WiFi. A sniffer can capture passwords, emails, and everything else sent unencrypted. The solution is encryption (TLS/HTTPS), but many protocols still send data in plaintext.

IP spoofing

The attacker crafts packets with a forged source IP address. The receiver believes the packet came from someone else. This is used in reflection attacks (DDoS), phishing, and to bypass access controls that are based on IP addresses.

07 · History

The history of the Internet

From ARPANET with four nodes to a network with billions of devices — in fifty years.

The history of the Internet is the history of an idea that proved unstoppable: that one could build a reliable communication system from unreliable parts, by dividing information into packets and letting them find their own way. The idea grew from military research into the world's most pervasive infrastructure.

The 1960s: the birth of packet switching

Three research groups — MIT/DARPA in the USA, NPL in England, and RAND in the USA — independently arrived at the idea of packet switching. In 1969, ARPANET was launched with four nodes (UCLA, SRI, UCSB, Utah). It was the world's first packet-switched network, and it worked.

The 1970s: more networks, the need for inter-networking

More packet-switched networks appeared: ALOHAnet (Hawaii, wireless), Telenet (commercial), Cyclades (France). The problem was that they couldn't talk to each other. Vinton Cerf and Bob Kahn designed TCP/IP to connect different networks — inter-networking — and published the architecture in 1974.

The 1980s: TCP/IP and DNS

January 1, 1983 — "flag day" — ARPANET officially switched to TCP/IP. DNS was introduced to translate names into IP addresses. The university networks (NSFNET) grew explosively, and email became the first "killer app."

The 1990s: the World Wide Web and commercialization

Tim Berners-Lee at CERN invented the World Wide Web in 1989–91: HTTP, HTML, and the first browser. The Mosaic browser (1993) and Netscape (1994) brought the web to the masses. NSFNET was decommissioned in 1995, and the Internet became fully commercialized. The dot-com boom brought enormous investments in network infrastructure.

The 2000s and beyond

Broadband replaced dial-up. WiFi and smartphones made the Internet mobile. Social media, cloud services, streaming services, and IoT exploded. Today over 5 billion people are connected to the Internet, and the volume of traffic roughly doubles every three years. The underlying protocols — TCP, IP, DNS, HTTP — are remarkably unchanged from when they were designed.

Reflection questions

Explain the difference between transmission delay and propagation delay using an analogy.

Show suggested answer

Think of a caravan of 10 cars that must pass through a toll booth and then drive 100 km to the next toll booth. Transmission delay corresponds to the time it takes for all the cars to pass through the booth (depends on how fast the booth processes cars — i.e., the link speed R and the number of bits L). Propagation delay corresponds to the time it takes for one car to drive the 100 km (depends on the distance and the speed of the signal in the medium). The two are completely independent: you can make the booth faster without shortening the road, and you can shorten the road without making the booth faster.

Why does the Internet use packet switching instead of circuit switching? Name at least one disadvantage of packet switching.

Show suggested answer

Packet switching is used because it utilizes network resources far more efficiently through statistical multiplexing: since users are rarely active at the same time, capacity can be shared dynamically. The same link can serve many more users than with circuit switching, where resources are reserved and sit unused when a user is idle.

Disadvantages: Packet switching provides no guarantees for delay or bandwidth — queuing delay is unpredictable, and packets can be lost (dropped) when router queues are full. This makes it harder to provide services that require real-time guarantees (e.g., traditional telephony).

What happens to queuing delay when the traffic intensity (La/R) approaches 1? And what happens when it exceeds 1?

Show suggested answer

When the traffic intensity approaches 1 (i.e., packets arrive almost as fast as they are sent out), the average queuing delay grows dramatically — it tends toward infinity. Small random variations in arrival times cause the queue to grow faster than it can drain.

When the traffic intensity exceeds 1, packets arrive faster than the link can send them out. The queue grows without bound — but since the buffer has a finite size, packets that don't fit are dropped (packet loss). Designing networks so that the traffic intensity stays well below 1 is therefore essential.
